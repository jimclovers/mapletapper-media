TREE ID BONUS: Norway maple. Can you tap it? 🍁

We promised the black maple next, and it's still coming. But first, here's a maple you've almost certainly walked past this week.

How to spot it:
🍃 Snap a leaf stem. Milky white sap means Norway. Sugar maples and other native maples run clear.
🍁 Wide leaves with sharp points that end in fine, hair-like tips
💛 Fall color is usually yellow
🌳 Bark with tight, criss-cross grooves
🌀 Seeds spread out almost flat (sugar maple seeds make a V)

Can you tap it? Yes! In a Utah State University study, Norway maple sap ran 2.2 to 2.8% sugar. That means about 35 gallons of sap make 1 gallon of syrup, close to sugar maple's 40. (Ohio State also says many tappers find it about as sweet as sugar maple.)

The catch is a slow drip: in that study, each tree gave only about 2 to 2½ gallons of sap all season.

Same rules as always: at least 12 inches across at 4½ feet up, a 5/16" tree-saver spout and food-grade gear. And if it's a street tree, ask your town first.

Good to know: Norway maple came from Europe in the mid-1700s. Today it's invasive in many states, because its deep shade and heavy seeding crowd out native trees, even sugar maple. Already have one? Tap it and enjoy it! Planting a new tree? Pick a native maple.
